Bending LEAR

The linear model's price curve is a straight line in residual load, but the market's merit order bends in two places: a floor just under zero and a steep wall on scarcity evenings. This page covers the three steps that test whether bending the line in those two places pays off, and how each step is scored.

STEP 1

A test bench that any model can use

Every model we try today gets the same two exams. Each exam catches something the other misses:

  • September exam (1–21 Sep 2026, 504 hours, official TSO forecasts as inputs). This is the regime that broke the live forecast: evening residual load of 54–57 GW and a slope three times steeper. Holding the inputs fixed means any change in score comes from the model, not from forecast noise.
  • Long exam (rolling backtest 2019–2026, ~66,000 hours). September has almost no negative hours and only 21 days, which is far too few to call a small gain real. The long exam is where negative prices and statistical significance get judged.

The bench is a function: give it a model and it returns the scores below. LEAR must reproduce its own reference numbers through it before we trust it with anything new.

The scores, one at a time

MAE and rMAE. MAE is the average absolute error in €/MWh. rMAE divides it by the MAE of a naive forecast ("same hour, one week ago"), so 0.41 means 59 % less error than copying last week. The ratio lets you compare calm years with crisis years. Minimising MAE drives a model toward the median price, which is why point models under-call spikes: a spike is never the median.
Pinball loss: MAE with a tilt

Pinball loss punishes under-forecasts with weight τ and over-forecasts with weight 1−τ. Averaged over many hours, it is minimised exactly at the τ-quantile of the price distribution. At τ = 0.5 it is half the MAE. At τ = 0.9 it rewards a forecast that sits above 90 % of outcomes. This is the loss the probabilistic models will train on. CRPS is pinball loss averaged over every τ from 0 to 1: one number that scores a whole predicted distribution, and it collapses to MAE when the "distribution" is a single point.

The Diebold–Mariano test: "is B really better than A, or did we get lucky?"

For each day, take model A's absolute error summed over the 24 hours, minus model B's. That gives one number per day, the loss differential d. If B is truly better, d averages above zero. The DM statistic is that average divided by its standard error (with a correction for days that are correlated with their neighbours). Far from zero means the gain is real, and |DM| > 1.96 is the usual 5 % bar. The simulation below draws daily differentials with a known true gain. Negative-hour scores (registered 2026-08-29). Sign recall: of the hours that cleared negative, the share the model also called negative (LEAR: 60 % backtest, 41 % live). Depth ratio: on hours where both were negative, the model's median divided by the actual median (LEAR: −15.0 vs −2.9, i.e. 5× too deep). A model that understands the zero floor should push recall up and the depth ratio toward 1 without losing MAE on positive hours.
STEP 2

Hinges: two bends in a straight line

"We tried residual load once, it didn't work." No run, config or report in any of the three repos shows residual load actually trained into a model; the closest is the residual-only explainer (reports/explainers/exog_input_vectors), marked untested. Either way, a linear residual load should not work, and that is the key to this step. LEAR already sees load L and renewables R separately, so adding L − R changes nothing:
βL·L + βR·R + γ·(L − R) = (βL+γ)·L + (βR−γ)·R   ← the same straight line, relabelled

A hinge is different: max(0, k − RL) is zero above the knot k and grows below it. No mix of L and R can produce that corner, so it hands the model a shape it had no way to express before. Two hinges give a line with two bends:

price ≈ a + b·RL + clow·max(0, klow − RL) + chigh·max(0, RL − khigh)

The low hinge lets the line flatten into the floor just under zero, where must-run units and §51 EEG curtailment bids pin the price. The high hinge lets it steepen on the scarcity wall. It stays a linear model in its weights, so LASSO, the rolling refit and every existing guarantee still apply. The plot below uses synthetic data shaped like the real merit order.

How it goes into the real model

  • Knots from the calibration window only. Each daily refit sets klow and khigh from residual-load quantiles of its own training days, so the knots never see the test day.
  • Same-day only (lag 0). Every extra input series normally costs 72 weights (3 lags × 24 h). Two hinges at all lags would take the model from 247 to 391 weights against 357 training days, back into the n < p regime that distorted the 364-day ablation. At lag 0 they cost 48 weights, giving 295.
  • Predictions written down before the fit, like every earlier experiment.
  • Pass condition: hours 16–18 in September improve on 39.5; on the long exam, sign recall and depth ratio improve with no MAE lost on positive hours, and DM confirms it against plain LEAR. If hinges get most of the way there, "this needs a neural net" goes into the plan as overstated.
STEP 3

Trees that correct LEAR

A gradient-boosted tree model (sklearn's HistGradientBoosting, as used by res-de and load-de) learns LEAR's error, not the price. The final forecast is LEAR plus the tree's correction. LEAR stays in charge of the price level. Trees can't extrapolate: a tree never predicts beyond values it saw in training, so on its own it could not follow a gas-price shock. A correction layer can still learn patterns like "high-RL evenings with a scarce margin: LEAR runs 40 low".

  • Target: LEAR's out-of-sample error from the rolling backtest. In-sample residuals are shrunk by the fit and would teach the trees that LEAR is better than it is.
  • Features: residual load and the hinges, hour, weekday, TTF gas and EUA carbon (2-day lag, not yet used by any model), LEAR's own forecast, and LEAR's recent errors.
  • Refit: expanding window, once a month, each month predicted only by a model trained on earlier months (the res-de loop).
Correction to what I told you this morning. I said LEAR's error from yesterday would leak. It doesn't: the prices for day D−1 come out of the D−2 auction, so they are public a full day before the D−1 12:00 gate. LEAR already uses them as its lag-1 input. Yesterday's LEAR error is fair game. The real trap is the in-sample residual described above.

Pass condition: beats the step-2 winner on both exams, significant under DM on the long one. This is also the bridge to probabilistic output: swap the tree's loss for pinball loss and the same machinery predicts quantiles.
