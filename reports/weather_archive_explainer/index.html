1 · What is in the archive

Each file is one 00Z run of the ECMWF ENS (the ensemble version of the Integrated Forecasting System, IFS), reduced to Germany. Not one forecast — 51 parallel forecasts of the same period, each a physically plausible version of tomorrow's weather. Six surface variables, chosen because each one maps onto a price mechanism:

VariableWhat it isUnits (stored)Why prices careAvailable since
u_100m, v_100m Wind vector at 100 m — hub height of modern turbines m/s Wind generation enters the merit order at ~zero marginal cost; windy days push gas out and prices down. The physically correct altitude for power. 2024-03-19
u_10m, v_10m Wind vector at 10 m (standard meteorological height) m/s Proxy for hub-height wind in the early era; strongly correlated but attenuated by surface friction. 2023-01-18 (start)
t_2m Air temperature at 2 m K Demand driver (heating/cooling), plus thermal-plant efficiency and river-cooling stress in heat waves. 2023-01-18 (start)
ssrd Surface solar radiation downwards, accumulated J/m² since forecast start — de-accumulate by differencing consecutive steps to get W/m² J/m² Solar PV generation; the midday price valley lives and dies by this number. 2024-03-06

Three eras, because the open-data catalogue grew

ECMWF's free feed added parameters over time, so the archive has eras. The model itself also improved underneath: native resolution jumped from 18 km to 9 km in June 2023, and the published grid from 0.4° to 0.25° in February 2024.

Red tick: 6 missing run dates (2023-04-27..05-02) — an upstream hole in ECMWF's S3 bucket, verified twice, unrecoverable. Everything else is complete and QA-verified (every file readable, ≥50 members, era-correct variable set).

2 · What we actually pulled (and what we deliberately didn't)

ECMWF publishes each 3-hourly forecast step as one global GRIB file of ~2.5 GB containing every variable and all members. Downloading that whole file ×10 steps ×1,301 dates would be ~25 TB. Instead, each GRIB ships with a .index sidecar listing the byte offset of every field — so we range-request only the six Germany-relevant surface fields:

→

→
1° cell meansper member, per step — regional structure kept, file size tamed
~1.4 MB / date

So one Parquet row is: (1° cell, member, valid time, variable) → value. We keep the full member dimension — no averaging across the ensemble at archive time. The distribution is the asset; collapsing it to a mean would throw away exactly the information a probabilistic model needs.

The timing constraint that shaped everything

The day-ahead auction closes at 12:00 CET on D−1. Any feature must be knowable before that gate — our hardest project rule (no leakage). That dictates which run and which steps:

  • Which run: the 00Z run of D−1, published ~07:00–08:00 UTC — comfortably before the gate. The 06Z run would be fresher but publishes too close to (or after) the gate, so it is excluded by design, not oversight.
  • Which steps: lead times +21 h to +48 h, 3-hourly. That window covers the delivery day D completely in UTC and in CET/CEST, including the 23/25-hour DST days.

Try this: drag the member slider down to 5 on the "most uncertain wind" day, then back up. With few members the spread estimate jumps around; with all 51 it stabilizes. That is the whole argument for paying the 51× storage cost — a distribution estimated from 5 samples is noise, from 51 it is usable.

ANN analogy. The ensemble is Monte-Carlo sampling at inference time — the same idea as MC-dropout or a deep ensemble. One forward pass gives a point estimate; 51 perturbed forward passes give samples from a predictive distribution. Spread across members ≈ predictive variance.

4 · Where the uncertainty comes from

The members differ because ECMWF injects noise at exactly the two places where its knowledge is imperfect:

What is perturbedHowANN analogy
1. Initial conditions The starting state of the atmosphere — never known exactly (finite observations, instrument error, gaps over oceans) An Ensemble of Data Assimilations (EDA) plus singular-vector perturbations — tiny nudges chosen in the directions where errors grow fastest over the next 48 h Perturbing the input, with the perturbations aligned to the most error-amplifying directions — adversarial-example logic, used for honesty instead of attack
2. Model error The physics the model cannot resolve (clouds, turbulence smaller than the 9 km grid) Stochastic physics (SPPT / SPP schemes): the parameterized tendencies are randomly jittered during the run, differently per member Dropout inside the network's hand-crafted layers at inference time — admitting the submodules themselves are approximations

Chaos does the rest: the atmosphere amplifies small differences exponentially (the butterfly effect, quantitatively real). You can see the amplification inside our own 27-hour window — spread at +48 h is systematically larger than at +21 h:

What this spread is — and is not — good for. We tested the obvious hypothesis: on days when the ensemble disagrees, is our price model's error larger? Pre-registered answer: no — partial rank correlation −0.002 (p=0.95) over 862 days, refuted in a registered study. Daily Germany-mean spread carries no incremental error signal. If ensemble information earns its keep in the neural phase, it will be as hourly/regional features scored with proper distributional rules (CRPS), not as a daily error dial.

5 · How the model underneath roughly works

The IFS is not a learned model — it is a hard-coded forward function built from physics, run at brutal scale. In ANN vocabulary:

The state vector

The atmosphere is discretized onto a global grid: ~9 km horizontal spacing, 137 vertical levels, with wind, temperature, humidity, pressure and cloud variables at every point — a state of order 10⁹ numbers. That is the "activation" the model evolves.

The forward pass

One timestep applies the discretized equations of fluid dynamics and thermodynamics (Navier–Stokes on a rotating sphere, plus radiation and moisture) to advance the state a few minutes. A 48-hour forecast is hundreds of these steps composed — a very deep residual network whose "weights" are the laws of physics and were never trained. The numerics (spectral transforms, semi-Lagrangian advection) exist to make each step stable and cheap.

The hand-crafted layers: parameterizations

Anything smaller than the grid — individual clouds, turbulent eddies, radiation passing through cloud droplets — cannot be resolved and is replaced by parameterization schemes: approximate, semi-empirical submodules that map the resolved state to the sub-grid effects. These are the model's weakest, most approximate parts — which is exactly why the stochastic-physics noise from section 4 is injected there and nowhere else.

The "training step": data assimilation

The one place gradient-based optimization genuinely appears. Every 12 hours, 4D-Var finds the initial state that best explains tens of millions of fresh observations (satellites, aircraft, radiosondes, ground stations) while staying close to the previous forecast. It minimizes a loss with two terms — misfit to observations plus deviation from the prior — and the gradients flow through the forecast model itself via an adjoint model, which is exactly backpropagation through the physics. But note what is optimized: the initial state, not the weights. The physics is fixed; only "where the atmosphere is right now" is fitted, fresh, every cycle.

The full loop, ANN-style. 4D-Var = optimizing the input embedding against a two-term loss (obs likelihood + prior), gradients via an adjoint (backprop). Forecast = a fixed, physics-defined forward pass composed hundreds of times. Ensemble = MC sampling over input noise and layer noise. The output we store = 51 samples from the predictive distribution of tomorrow's weather over Germany.

Aside: ECMWF now also operates AIFS — an actually-learned neural forecast model running alongside the physics IFS, trained on decades of reanalysis. Our archive is the physics ENS; the fact that a learned model is becoming competitive with a 10⁹-state physics integrator is one of the most interesting stories in the field.
