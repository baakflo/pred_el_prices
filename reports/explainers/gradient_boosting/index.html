Gradient boosting & LightGBM — trees doing gradient descent

The one idea: in a neural net you do gradient descent on the weights; in boosting you do gradient descent on the function itself. Start from a constant prediction, compute the gradient of the loss at every training point, fit a small tree to that gradient, and take a step:

$$F_m(x) \;=\; F_{m-1}(x)\;+\;\eta\, h_m(x) \qquad\text{where}\qquad h_m \;\approx\; -\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}\big(y,\,F_{m-1}\big)}{\partial F_{m-1}}$$

Each tree is one gradient step; \(\eta\) is the learning rate. For squared error the negative gradient is just the residual \(y - F(x)\) — "fit a tree to what's left of the error, add it, repeat." The final model is a sum of hundreds of small trees.

What to try

  • Step one tree at a time at η = 0.3: the first trees grab the biggest structure (evening spike), later trees make ever-smaller corrections — the green steps shrink toward zero as the gradient vanishes.
  • η = 1.0, 4 leaves: fast fit, jagged model. η = 0.05: takes ~10× more trees for the same train loss but the curve is smoother — small-lr-long-schedule, same intuition as in deep learning.
  • 10 leaves + η = 1.0 + 100 trees: watch the test curve lift off the train curve. That gap is why num_leaves is LightGBM's main capacity knob and why you always early-stop on a validation set.
  • Sliders apply to future trees — you can anneal η mid-run by hand.

2 · The pinball loss, up close — why asymmetry finds a quantile

Forget features and models for a minute. Below are 48 price draws for one delivery hour — one number per day, the way reality hands them to you, no repeats, no histogram. You publish a single prediction \(f\), and each realized price charges you an asymmetric fee:

$$\mathcal{L}_\tau(y, f) \;=\; \begin{cases} \tau\,(y-f) & y \ge f \quad\text{— you were low: pay } \tau \text{ per EUR of miss}\\[2pt] (1-\tau)\,(f-y) & y < f \quad\text{— you were high: pay } 1-\tau \text{ per EUR of miss} \end{cases}$$

Think tug-of-war: every point above your prediction pulls it up with force τ, every point below pulls it down with force 1−τ. "Pull" is nothing mystical — raise \(f\) by 1 EUR and each point above you charges τ less (its miss shrank by 1), while each point below charges 1−τ more (its miss grew by 1). A pull is just each point's vote on which direction makes your bill cheaper. Set the total bill's slope to zero:

$$\frac{d}{df}\sum_i \mathcal{L}_\tau(y_i, f) \;=\; (1-\tau)\,n_{\text{below}} \;-\; \tau\,n_{\text{above}} \;=\; 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{n_{\text{below}}}{N} \;=\; \tau$$

The equilibrium depends only on how many points sit on each side — not where they are, not what distribution they came from. So the cheapest \(f\) is the value with a fraction τ of the data below it: the τ-quantile, by definition. Single draws are enough supervision for a whole distribution — the asymmetry does the counting.

Dots: the 48 observed prices. Bars: each point's current charge — red where the price landed above your f (you were low, fee τ/EUR), blue where below (you were high, fee (1−τ)/EUR). Drag the orange line and try to make the total bill small, then press gradient-descend and watch f settle where the pulls balance.

The total bill \(\sum_i \mathcal{L}_\tau(y_i, f)\) as a function of where you put f. Black ▼: the minimum. Dashed blue: the empirical τ-quantile of the sample. They coincide at every τ — that's the theorem, drawn.

What to try

  • τ = 0.5: both fees are 0.5/EUR and the optimum is the median. Drag f a few EUR off-optimum: the bill barely moves as long as the counts don't change — the slope only knows headcounts.
  • τ = 0.9, drag f from low to high: while more than 10% of the points are above f, the red up-pull (τ each) beats the blue down-pull (0.1 each), so the bill keeps falling as you raise f. It bottoms out when ~5 of 48 points remain above. Push τ to 0.95 and watch the optimum jump into the spike cluster.
  • Press gradient-descend from far away: the step direction only ever depends on the count imbalance — this ±τ / ±(1−τ) push is exactly the gradient the boosted trees of panel 3 are fitted to, computed per leaf instead of globally.
  • The tail-data problem, live: at τ = 0.95 the optimum is held in place by just 2–3 points. Nudge τ and it lurches — that's why extreme-quantile heads are noisy, and why the parametric (Johnson's SU) rival is interesting in the tails.

3 · Swap the loss: pinball → quantile forecasts

Now combine panels 1 and 2: the boosting machinery never used "squared error" specifically — only its gradient. And the pinball gradient is exactly the tug-of-war force from above:

$$-\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_\tau}{\partial f} \;=\; \begin{cases} \tau & y \ge f\\[2pt] \tau - 1 & y < f \end{cases}$$

Fit each tree to that ±force instead of the residual, and every leaf replays panel 2's equilibrium locally, conditioned on features: the model settles where a fraction \(\tau\) of the training points sit below it in each region of feature space — a conditional quantile. Train several \(\tau\)'s and you have a predictive distribution.

Toy market: x = wind forecast, y = price. Low wind → high, volatile, spike-prone prices; high wind → low, occasionally negative prices (the noise is heteroscedastic, so the quantile band should widen and narrow). Blue/black/red: boosted q10/q50/q90. Orange dashed: your τ — drag the slider.

What to try

  • Check the coverage readout: a q90 model is calibrated iff ~90% of the dots sit below its curve. That's the same "empirical coverage" number the Phase-2 harness will score for real.
  • Slide τ from 0.05 to 0.95: the curve sweeps through the point cloud. Notice the q10–q90 band is wide at low wind (spiky regime) and narrow at high wind — the model learned heteroscedasticity with no explicit variance head, just two asymmetric losses.
  • τ = 0.5 is median regression — already more robust to the price spikes than the squared-loss fit of panel 1 would be.

4 · What makes LightGBM "Light": leaf-wise growth

Classic implementations (XGBoost's original mode) grow trees level-wise: split every leaf, then every leaf of the next depth — balanced trees. LightGBM grows leaf-wise: always split whichever leaf, anywhere in the tree, gives the largest loss reduction — greedy steepest descent over splits. Same leaf budget, deeper structure where the function actually bends. (The other speed tricks: histogram binning — quantize each feature to ≤255 bins and scan bins, not thresholds; GOSS — keep all large-gradient rows, subsample the easy ones, i.e. importance-sample the batch by loss.)

A merit-order toy surface: price as a function of residual load (x) and hour (y). Price climbs gently, then kinks steeply when expensive plants set the margin; an evening-peak premium only applies at high residual load (an interaction); negative prices when RES floods the market. Both trees get the same leaf budget on the same noisy sample of this surface.
